Analog Wave-Based Neural Architectures: A New Paradigm

Author: Justinas (conceptual lead), GitHub Copilot (scribe)

Abstract

Traditional neural networks rely on discrete values, rigid layer structures, and fully connected architectures. This paper proposes a biologically inspired alternative: a wave-based, analog neural system with spatial dimensionality and local connectivity. By leveraging sine wave compositions, 3D topologies, and STL-like data representations, we aim to create a more fluid, adaptive, and efficient model of artificial cognition.

1. Introduction

Modern AI systems are built on digital abstractions — matrices, tensors, and activation functions. Yet biological intelligence operates in a continuous, analog domain. Neurons fire in rhythms, not bits. Signals propagate through space, not layers. This paper explores what happens when we abandon hard values and embrace waves, space, and structure.

2. Wave-Based Encoding

Instead of representing data as static numbers, we propose encoding inputs and weights as composite waveforms. Using sine, cosine, and other periodic functions, we can shape signal profiles that carry richer information. This allows for:

  • Phase-based modulation
  • Frequency domain learning
  • Analog interference patterns for decision-making

3. Spatial Dimensionality

Neurons are not flat. They exist in space. We propose modeling networks in 3D topologies where each neuron has a position, length, and local environment. Long neurons (e.g. sensory pathways) are rare and specialized. Most connections should be local, mimicking cortical microcircuits.

4. STL-Like Data Representation

To encode the network structure, we suggest using a format inspired by .stl files — commonly used in 3D modeling. Each crease or vertex could represent a weight or connection. This allows for:

  • Physical modeling of neural geometry
  • Simulation of signal propagation through space
  • Integration with CAD tools for visualization

5. Biological Parallels

This architecture aligns with known biological principles:

  • Local connectivity and sparse activation
  • Wave-based signaling (e.g. brain oscillations)
  • Spatial organization of function (e.g. somatosensory maps)

6. Future Directions

We envision a system where learning occurs through wave interference, spatial adaptation, and analog feedback loops. This could lead to:

  • More energy-efficient AI
  • Better generalization and robustness
  • Closer alignment with biological intelligence
“We need to stop forcing all data through every neuron and start letting the network choose its own path — like a brain.”

7. Conclusion

This is not just a new architecture — it's a new way of thinking. By embracing analog signals, spatial structure, and biological realism, we may unlock a deeper form of artificial intelligence.
